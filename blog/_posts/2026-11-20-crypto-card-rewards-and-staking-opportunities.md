<p>Crypto card rewards usually come in one of two forms: cashback paid in a token, or a cashback rate that goes up the more of that card issuer's token you lock up (stake). Staking opportunities are separate from the card itself — they're a condition attached to earning the higher reward tier, and they carry their own risks that a plain cashback card doesn't have.</p>

<h2>How crypto card rewards actually work</h2>
<p>Most stablecoin and crypto debit or credit cards pay rewards in one of three ways:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flat cashback</strong> paid in a stablecoin — the simplest structure, because the value of what you earn doesn't swing with the market.</li>
<li><strong>Cashback paid in the issuer's native token</strong> — the percentage can look generous, but the value of the reward moves with that token's price, so what you redeem may be worth more or less than it looked when you earned it.</li>
<li><strong>Tiered cashback tied to staking</strong> — you lock a certain amount of the issuer's token for a set period, and in exchange you unlock a better spending rate, airport lounge access, or fee waivers.</li>
</ul>
<p>The tiered-staking model is where most people get confused, because the headline reward rate often assumes you've already locked up a meaningful balance of a token you may not otherwise want to hold.</p>

<h2>What to check before you stake anything for a card</h2>
<p>Staking to unlock a card tier is a real financial commitment, not a feature toggle. Before locking funds, it's worth working through:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lock-up length.</strong> Staking periods can run from a few months to a year or longer. Find out what happens if you need that money back early.</li>
<li><strong>What you're staking, not what you're spending.</strong> A card can offer stablecoin spending day to day while requiring you to stake a separate, more volatile token to earn the top tier. These are two different risk profiles living on one card.</li>
<li><strong>Custody model.</strong> Some issuers hold your staked tokens themselves (custodial); others route the stake through a smart contract you control (non-custodial, or self-custody). This affects who can freeze or seize the stake and how recovery works if the issuer has problems.</li>
<li><strong>Whether the reward tier can be downgraded.</strong> Issuers can and do change staking requirements and reward tiers. Read how the program describes changes to existing stakers, not just new sign-ups.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Stablecoin rewards vs. token rewards</h2>
<p>A stablecoin reward of a given size stays close to that size. A token reward of the same stated size can be worth noticeably more or less by the time you actually use it, depending on market conditions at redemption. Neither is automatically better — a token reward can appreciate — but they are not the same kind of reward, and comparison tools should show them separately rather than blending them into one number.</p>

<h2>Reading the fine print on "up to X%" rewards</h2>
<p>Advertised top rates are almost always the ceiling, reachable only at the highest staking tier. The rate most cardholders actually earn tends to be considerably lower. When comparing cards, look for the full tier table — stake amount required at each level, and the reward rate at each level — rather than the single number in the marketing headline.</p>

<h2>Custodial vs. non-custodial cards, in plain terms</h2>
<p>A custodial card means the issuer holds your funds and your staked tokens on your behalf, similar to how a bank holds a deposit. A non-custodial or self-custody card means your funds sit in a wallet you control, and the card draws against that wallet. Custodial setups are usually simpler to use but depend on the issuer's own solvency and policies. Self-custody setups put more control — and more responsibility for key management — in your hands. Neither structure is right for everyone; it depends on how much control you want versus how much convenience you're trading for it.</p>

<h2>Questions worth asking before you commit</h2>
<ul>
<li>Is the reward paid in a stablecoin or in the issuer's own token?</li>
<li>What's the full staking tier table, not just the top rate?</li>
<li>Is the card custodial or self-custody, and what does that mean if the issuer runs into trouble?</li>
<li>Can the issuer change reward tiers for people already staked in?</li>
<li>What happens to your staked balance if you want to exit early?</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common questions</h2>
<h3>What does it mean to stake for a crypto card reward tier?</h3>
<p>It means locking up a certain amount of the card issuer's token for a set period in exchange for a higher cashback rate or extra perks; the lock-up length and what happens if you need the funds early vary by issuer.</p>
<h3>Are crypto card rewards paid in stablecoins or in volatile tokens?</h3>
<p>It depends on the card — some pay cashback in a stablecoin that holds a steady value, others pay in the issuer's own token, which can be worth more or less than it looked when you earned it by the time you redeem it.</p>
