<p>A stablecoin debit card lets you spend crypto that's pegged to a currency like the US dollar — usually USDC or USDT — at any merchant that takes Visa or Mastercard. The card converts your stablecoin balance at the point of sale, so you're spending digital dollars without needing the merchant to accept crypto directly.</p>

<h2>How a stablecoin card actually works</h2>
<p>You load or hold stablecoins in a linked wallet or account. When you tap or swipe, the card network converts the stablecoin to local currency in real time and settles with the merchant as if you'd used a regular debit card. Because the underlying asset is pegged to a fiat currency, the amount you see on your statement should match what left your wallet, minus any network or conversion cost. This is the main reason people choose stablecoins over volatile cryptocurrencies for everyday spending — the balance isn't supposed to swing in value while you're deciding what to buy.</p>

<h2>Custodial vs non-custodial: the distinction that actually matters</h2>
<p>Every stablecoin card falls into one of two custody models, and this is the single most important thing to understand before signing up.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Custodial cards</strong> are issued by a company that holds your stablecoins on your behalf, similar to how a bank holds deposits. You trust that company's solvency and security practices.</li>
<li><strong>Non-custodial (self-custody) cards</strong> connect to a wallet you control. The issuer never holds your funds directly; spending pulls from your own wallet at the moment of transaction.</li>
</ul>
<p>Neither model is automatically safer. Custodial cards can offer more consumer protections and easier recovery if something goes wrong, but you're relying on a third party. Non-custodial cards put you in control of your funds, but that also means you're responsible for your own wallet security and recovery phrases. Anyone comparing cards should know which model each one uses before applying.</p>

<h2>What else to check before choosing a card</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Which blockchain the stablecoin lives on.</strong> USDC and USDT exist on several different chains, and not every card supports all of them — this affects how you top up and withdraw.</li>
<li><strong>Foreign exchange and conversion costs.</strong> Even with a stablecoin, there can be a spread or fee when converting to local currency at checkout.</li>
<li><strong>Rewards structure.</strong> Some cards pay rewards in the stablecoin itself, others in a separate token, and some require you to stake or lock up funds to unlock higher reward tiers. Staking requirements are worth reading carefully — they change what "free" rewards actually cost you in flexibility.</li>
<li><strong>Where the card is issued and where it works.</strong> Card operations differ by market — some cards are only available in certain regions, or work differently depending on local banking rules.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common questions</h2>
<h3>What is a stablecoin debit card?</h3>
<p>It's a debit card linked to stablecoins like USDC or USDT that converts your balance to local currency at checkout, so you can spend digital dollars anywhere Visa or Mastercard is accepted.</p>
<h3>Is a stablecoin debit card safe to use?</h3>
<p>Safety depends mainly on whether the card is custodial or non-custodial — custodial cards rely on the issuer holding your funds securely, while non-custodial cards rely on you securing your own wallet.</p>
<h3>What's the difference between custodial and non-custodial stablecoin cards?</h3>
<p>Custodial cards have a company holding your stablecoins on your behalf, while non-custodial cards pull funds directly from a wallet you control at the moment you spend.</p>
